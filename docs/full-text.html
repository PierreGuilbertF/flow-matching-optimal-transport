Flow Matching and Optimal Transport: An Illustrated Introduction to Wasserstein–Otto Geometry

Pierre Guilbert · 2026 · 0009-0002-1582-4435

Download the PDF. This HTML version is provided for reading and indexing; the PDF is the reference version.

Abstract

We recall and derive the equations underlying Flow Matching, making explicit the probability density function of the linearly interpolated random variable and relating it to the transporting velocity field through the continuity equation. We visualise how the initial density is transported and compare the resulting path with a geodesic in Wasserstein–Otto geometry. We demonstrate that, for an isotropic Gaussian initial law, the Flow Matching velocity field is the minimum-energy representative of the density variation at each time, among fields satisfying the continuity equation, even though the path is generally not a geodesic under the Wasserstein–Otto metric. We explain the distinction between this local minimisation and the minimisation of the action over paths with fixed endpoints using explicit calculations and illustrations.

Keywords: Flow Matching; optimal transport; Wasserstein–Otto geometry; generative modelling; continuity equation; Wasserstein geodesics.

1 Introduction

Generative machine learning consists in approximating a probability density pdatap_{\mathrm{data}}, from independent and identically distributed samples x1,…,xN∼pdatax_1,\ldots,x_N\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}, using a model pθp_\theta. A classical approach is to estimate an energy-based parametric model pθ(x)=Zθ−1e−Uθ(x),Zθ=∫Ee−Uθ(x)dx,p_\theta(x)=Z_\theta^{-1}e^{-U_\theta(x)},\qquad Z_\theta=\int_E e^{-U_\theta(x)}\,dx, whose parameters are optimised by minimising the Kullback–Leibler divergence DKL(pdata∥pθ)D_{\mathrm{KL}}(p_{\mathrm{data}}\|p_\theta), or another discrepancy. This approach and the difficulties arising from it are discussed in Mallat’s lectures at the Collège de France [18]. Computing the normalising constant ZθZ_\theta and the model expectations appearing in likelihood-based training involves integrals which are generally intractable in high dimension, as for images or videos. Even after training, samples must be drawn from the model. For energy-based models, this is often done with Markov chain Monte Carlo methods, such as the Metropolis algorithm [19].

A recent approach combines distribution estimation and sampling by learning how to transport a simple distribution pinitp_{\mathrm{init}} to the data distribution. Diffusion models [20], [21] progressively add noise to data until the resulting distribution is close to a prescribed noise law. A learned reverse process then generates data by progressively removing this noise. Flow Matching [2] instead learns a velocity field associated with a chosen probability path. In the case studied here, this path results from linear interpolation between a random variable drawn from the data distribution and an independent random variable drawn from the initial law. Sampling is performed by integrating this field from the initial distribution.

This manuscript takes a pedagogical point of view on the probability path and velocity field arising from linear interpolation in Flow Matching. We derive the density of the interpolated random variable, relate its evolution to the transporting velocity through the continuity equation, and illustrate the resulting flow. We then use Wasserstein–Otto geometry to address two questions. At each time, is the Flow Matching velocity a minimum-energy field transporting the prescribed density variation? Does the density path itself follow a geodesic between its endpoints? Separating these questions helps explain the role of the velocity field and of the chosen probability path in Flow Matching. We discuss them through explicit calculations and a Gaussian mixture example.

2 Flow Matching and transport of densities

Flow Matching belongs to the family of methods which estimate the probability distribution underlying the available data by transporting an initial distribution pinitp_{\mathrm{init}}. The idea is to approximate pdatap_{\mathrm{data}} by the push-forward of pinitp_{\mathrm{init}} under a flow ϕt\phi_t associated with a velocity field vθ(t,x)v_\theta(t,x). For each initial point xx, its trajectory t↦ϕt(x)t\mapsto\phi_t(x) satisfies ∂tϕt(x)=vθ(t,ϕt(x)),ϕ0(x)=x.\partial_t\phi_t(x)=v_\theta(t,\phi_t(x)), \qquad \phi_0(x)=x. The flow transports the initial distribution, and its value at t=1t=1 defines the model distribution: pdatady≃(ϕ1)#(pinitdx).p_{\mathrm{data}}\,dy\simeq(\phi_1)_\#(p_{\mathrm{init}}\,dx). The velocity field vθv_\theta is to be learned. Flow Matching represents it by a neural network and learns it by regression, without differentiating through the numerical solution of the flow equation [2]. Related formulations include rectified flow [7]. They have been used for high-resolution image synthesis [8], smaller one-step image generators [9], and image editing [10]. Applications also include speech generation with Voicebox [11], speech and sound generation with Audiobox [12], video and audio generation with Movie Gen [13], and audio-driven portrait animation with FLOAT [14].

2.1 Training and sampling

2.1.0.1 Training.

We use the data-to-initial time convention for training, and reverse it when sampling. A neural network vθ(t,y)v_\theta(t,y) represents the velocity in this convention. Flow Matching allows several choices of conditional probability paths; here we use independent X∼pdataX\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}, Z∼pinitZ\sim p_{\mathrm{init}} and the linear interpolation Xt=(1−t)X+tZ,Ẋt=Z−X.X_t=(1-t)X+tZ,\qquad \dot X_t=Z-X. With tt sampled uniformly on [0,1][0,1], independently of (X,Z)(X,Z), the conditional regression loss is ℒ(θ)=𝔼t,X,Z[∥vθ(t,(1−t)X+tZ)−(Z−X)∥2].\begin{equation} \label{eq:fm-training-loss} \mathcal L(\theta) =\mathbb E_{t,X,Z}\![ \|v_\theta(t,(1-t)X+tZ)-(Z-X)\|^2]. \end{equation}(1) Training is relatively simple: evaluating the expectation requires sampling ZZ from pinitp_{\mathrm{init}}, drawing XX from the available data, and sampling tt uniformly in [0,1][0,1]. These draws are independent. The loss is estimated on minibatches and θ\theta is updated by stochastic gradient methods. The target Z−XZ-X is directly available, so no ODE simulation is needed during training. The linear interpolation is one choice within the conditional Flow Matching framework [2].

At the population level, minimisation over square-integrable fields gives vt(y)=𝔼[Z−X∣Xt=y].\begin{equation} \label{eq:fm-population-target} v_t(y)=\mathbb E[Z-X\mid X_t=y]. \end{equation}(2) This follows from the conditional-mean characterisation of least-squares regression.

2.1.0.2 Sampling.

We draw Y0∼pinitY_0\sim p_{\mathrm{init}} and integrate the learned velocity in reverse time: dYsds=−vθ(1−s,Ys),0≤s≤1.\frac{dY_s}{ds}=-v_\theta(1-s,Y_s),\qquad 0\leq s\leq1. The generative flow ϕs\phi_s introduced above therefore uses the velocity −vθ(1−s,⋅)-v_\theta(1-s,\cdot), and its endpoint gives the model distribution (ϕ1)#(pinitdx)(\phi_1)_\#(p_{\mathrm{init}}\,dx). In practice, the integration is performed with a numerical solver, such as explicit Euler or a Runge–Kutta method.

2.2 Velocity fields and density evolution

Consider a smooth velocity field vtv_t and its flow ϕt\phi_t, with ∂tϕt=vt∘ϕt\partial_t\phi_t=v_t\circ\phi_t and ϕ0=Id\phi_0=\operatorname{Id}. Assume that this flow is a diffeomorphism and that ptp_t is a smooth transported density. For a bounded domain Ω\Omega with smooth boundary, define the moving domain Ωt=ϕt(Ω).\Omega_t=\phi_t(\Omega). Preservation of mass gives ∫Ωtpt(y)dy=∫Ωp0(x)dx.\int_{\Omega_t}p_t(y)\,dy=\int_{\Omega}p_0(x)\,dx. Differentiating with Reynolds’ transport theorem yields 0=ddt∫Ωtpt(y)dy=∫Ωt(∂tpt+div⁡(ptvt))(y)dy.0=\frac{d}{dt}\int_{\Omega_t}p_t(y)\,dy =\int_{\Omega_t}\bigl(\partial_t p_t+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(p_tv_t)\bigr)(y)\,dy. Since this holds for every such domain, we obtain the continuity equation ∂tpt+div⁡(ptvt)=0.\partial_t p_t+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(p_tv_t)=0.

This equation links the temporal derivative of the density ptp_t to the velocity field transporting it. The Wasserstein–Otto metric will rely on this relation to measure the kinetic cost of a density variation; we return to it in Section 4.

3 Probability paths under independent interpolation

We consider a Euclidean space E≃ℝdE\simeq\mathbb{R}^d, with its Lebesgue measure, and two densities pdata,pinit∈𝒫+(E)p_{\mathrm{data}},p_{\mathrm{init}}\in\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) with finite second moments. Let XX and ZZ be independent random variables with densities pdatap_{\mathrm{data}} and pinitp_{\mathrm{init}}, respectively. We study the interpolation Xt=(1−t)X+tZ,0≤t≤1.\begin{equation} \label{eq:data-noise-interpolation} X_t=(1-t)X+tZ,\qquad 0\leq t\leq1. \end{equation}(3) Here time runs from the data distribution to the initial distribution: p0=pdatap_0=p_{\mathrm{data}} and p1=pinitp_1=p_{\mathrm{init}}. The generative direction is obtained by reversing time. Independence is part of the construction; a different coupling of the same endpoints may give a different density path.

3.1 Contracted densities and convolution

The interpolation map and its fibres. For fixed tt, introduce the linear map ψt:E×E→E,ψt(x,z)=(1−t)x+tz.\psi_t:E\times E\longrightarrow E,\qquad \psi_t(x,z)=(1-t)x+tz. The law of XtX_t is the push-forward of the product measure pdata(x)pinit(z)dxdzp_{\mathrm{data}}(x)p_{\mathrm{init}}(z)\,dx\,dz by ψt\psi_t. In orthonormal coordinates its differential has matrix Jt:=Dψt=Mat⁡(dψt)=((1−t)IdtId).J_t:=D\psi_t=\mathop{\mathrm{Mat}}(d\psi_t) =\begin{pmatrix}(1-t)\operatorname{Id}&t\operatorname{Id}\end{pmatrix}. Thus ψt\psi_t is surjective, and its normal volume factor is At:=det⁡(JtJt𝖳)=((1−t)2+t2)d/2.\begin{equation} \label{eq:normal-jacobian} A_t:=\sqrt{\det(J_tJ_t^{\mathsf T})} =\bigl((1-t)^2+t^2\bigr)^{d/2}. \end{equation}(4) The density pt=pXtp_t=p_{X_t} is obtained by integrating the joint density of (X,Z)(X,Z) over the fibre ψt−1(y)\psi_t^{-1}(y). Applying the coarea formula to this push-forward [1], we obtain pt(y)=1At∫ψt−1(y)pdata(x)pinit(z)dℋd(x,z).\begin{equation} \label{eq:fibre-density} p_t(y)=\frac1{A_t} \int_{\psi_t^{-1}(y)}p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)p_{\mathrm{init}}(z)\, d\mathcal H^d(x,z). \end{equation}(5) Here ℋd\mathcal H^d is the induced Euclidean volume on the fibre, and At>0A_t>0 is the constant normal volume factor computed in (4).

For 0<t<10<t<1, parametrise the fibre by Ft,y(x)=(x,y−(1−t)xt).F_{t,y}(x)=(x,\frac{y-(1-t)x}{t}). Writing DFt,yDF_{t,y} for its Jacobian matrix in orthonormal coordinates, its induced volume element is det⁡((DFt,y)𝖳DFt,y)dx=(1+(1−t)2t2)d/2dx=Attddx.\sqrt{\det\!((DF_{t,y})^{\mathsf T}DF_{t,y})}\,dx =(1+\frac{(1-t)^2}{t^2})^{d/2}dx =\frac{A_t}{t^d}\,dx. Substitution into (5) cancels AtA_t and yields pt(y)=∫Epdata(x)t−dpinit(y−(1−t)xt)dx.\begin{equation} \label{eq:convolution-integral} p_t(y)=\int_E p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)\,t^{-d} p_{\mathrm{init}}\!(\frac{y-(1-t)x}{t})dx. \end{equation}(6)

Convolution form. Rescaling the integration variable in (6) gives pt=[1(1−t)dpdata(⋅1−t)]*[1tdpinit(⋅t)],0<t<1.\begin{equation} \label{eq:contracted-convolution} \boxed{\, p_t= [\frac{1}{(1-t)^d}\,p_{\mathrm{data}}\!(\frac{\cdot}{1-t})] * [\frac{1}{t^d}\,p_{\mathrm{init}}\!(\frac{\cdot}{t})], \qquad 0<t<1. \,} \end{equation}(7) The initial and data distributions are contracted by tt and 1−t1-t, respectively, and then convolved. When tt is close to zero, pdatap_{\mathrm{data}} is almost unchanged, while the contracted initial distribution is close to a Dirac mass at the origin. The convolution is therefore close to pdatap_{\mathrm{data}}. When tt is close to one, we have the opposite phenomenon: the contracted data distribution is close to a Dirac mass at the origin, while pinitp_{\mathrm{init}} is almost unchanged. The convolution is then close to pinitp_{\mathrm{init}}. Figure 2 illustrates this evolution.

3.2 The initial distribution and the marginal velocity

For 0<t<10<t<1, assume that the endpoint densities are smooth and rapidly decreasing, so that we may differentiate under the integral in (6). Directly, ∂tpt(y)=∫Epdata(x)∂t[1tdpinit(y−(1−t)xt)]dx=∫Epdata(x)[−dtd+1pinit(y−(1−t)xt)+1td⟨∇pinit(y−(1−t)xt),x−yt2⟩]dx.\begin{align} \partial_t p_t(y) &=\int_E p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)\, \partial_t\![ \frac1{t^d}p_{\mathrm{init}}\!(\frac{y-(1-t)x}{t}) ]dx\\ &=\int_E p_{\mathrm{data}}(x)[ -\frac{d}{t^{d+1}}p_{\mathrm{init}}\!(\frac{y-(1-t)x}{t}) \\ & +\frac1{t^d}\langle \nabla p_{\mathrm{init}}\!(\frac{y-(1-t)x}{t}), \frac{x-y}{t^2}\rangle ]dx. \label{eq:density-time-derivative} \end{align}(8)

4 Wasserstein–Otto geometry and comparison with geodesics

For a broader comparison of Fisher–Rao and Wasserstein–Otto geometries, we refer to [23].

We keep the same endpoints pdatap_{\mathrm{data}} and pinitp_{\mathrm{init}}, with finite second moments, and the same time direction. We now describe what would be required of a Wasserstein–Otto geodesic between them. Write ptgeop_t^{\mathrm{geo}} for this reference path, to distinguish it from the independently coupled interpolation studied above.

4.1 Tangent space of probability densities

We briefly recall the density representation of probability measures. On E≃ℝdE\simeq\mathbb{R}^d, a probability measure PP absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure ℒd\mathcal L^d admits a Radon–Nikodym density p=dP/dℒdp=dP/d\mathcal L^d, so that [15] (Chapter 3) P(A)=∫Ap(x)dx,∫Ep(x)dx=1P(A)=\int_A p(x)\,dx,\qquad \int_E p(x)\,dx=1 for every Borel set A⊂EA\subset E. We work with the space of strictly positive probability densities 𝒫+(E)={p>0:∫Ep(x)dx=1},\mathcal{P}_{+}(E)=\{p>0:\ \int_E p(x)\,dx=1\}, with finite second moments and the regularity required by the following computations. In the formal smooth description, a vector in the tangent space Tp𝒫+(E)T_p\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) is a density variation uu with ∫Eudx=0\int_Eu\,dx=0; along a differentiable probability path, u=∂tptu=\partial_t p_t at the corresponding time [4].

4.2 Nonuniqueness of the transporting velocity

For a prescribed density path ptp_t, the continuity equation determines div⁡(ptvt)\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(p_tv_t). Several velocity fields may have the same weighted divergence [4], [16]. Indeed, if vtv_t satisfies the continuity equation and wtw_t satisfies div⁡(ptwt)=0,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(p_tw_t)=0, then ∂tpt+div⁡(pt(vt+wt))=∂tpt+div⁡(ptvt)+div⁡(ptwt)=0.\partial_t p_t+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}\bigl(p_t(v_t+w_t)\bigr) =\partial_t p_t+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(p_tv_t)+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(p_tw_t)=0. Conversely, the difference of two velocity fields representing the same density variation has zero weighted divergence. Among admissible fields, the WO construction selects a representative of minimum kinetic energy [4], [16].

4.3 The Wasserstein–Otto metric and path energy

The Wasserstein–Otto metric acts on these tangent vectors. Using the continuity equation, its squared norm along a smooth density path is [4], [16] ∥∂tpt∥pt,WO2=infv:div⁡(ptv)=−∂tpt∫E∥v(y)∥2pt(y)dy.\begin{equation} \label{eq:wo-tangent-recall} \boxed{\, \|\partial_t p_t\|_{p_t,\mathrm{WO}}^2 =\inf_{v:\,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(p_tv)=-\partial_t p_t} \int_E\|v(y)\|^2p_t(y)\,dy. \,} \end{equation}(11)

4.3.0.1 Weighted Helmholtz decomposition.

For vector fields in L2(p;E)L^2(p;E), consider the scalar product ⟨u,v⟩L2(p)=∫Eu(x)⋅v(x)p(x)dx.\langle u,v\rangle_{L^2(p)}=\int_E u(x)\cdot v(x)\,p(x)\,dx. The weighted Helmholtz decomposition splits a field orthogonally into v=∇ϕ+w,div⁡(pw)=0,⟨∇ϕ,w⟩L2(p)=0;v=\nabla\phi+w,\qquad \mathop{\mathrm{div}}(pw)=0,\qquad \langle\nabla\phi,w\rangle_{L^2(p)}=0; see [17] (Section 6). In the general L2(p)L^2(p) setting, the gradient component belongs to the closure of smooth compactly supported gradients, and the divergence condition is distributional. We use ∇ϕ\nabla\phi for this component in the smooth setting. Consequently, ∫E∥v∥2pdx=∫E∥∇ϕ∥2pdx+∫E∥w∥2pdx.\int_E\|v\|^2p\,dx =\int_E\|\nabla\phi\|^2p\,dx+\int_E\|w\|^2p\,dx. Applied at each time with p=ptp=p_t, this decomposition identifies the minimiser in (11): the gradient component represents the same density variation and has minimum kinetic energy [17].

For a full path t↦ptt\mapsto p_t, 0≤t≤10\leq t\leq1, its energy is [16] ℰWO(p)=∫01∥∂tpt∥pt,WO2dt=∫01infv:div⁡(ptv)=−∂tpt∫E∥v(y)∥2pt(y)dydt.\begin{equation} \label{eq:wo-path-energy} \mathcal E_{\mathrm{WO}}(p) =\int_0^1\|\partial_t p_t\|_{p_t,\mathrm{WO}}^2\,dt =\int_0^1\inf_{v:\,\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(p_tv)=-\partial_t p_t} \int_E\|v(y)\|^2p_t(y)\,dy\,dt. \end{equation}(12)

4.4 Minimum-energy velocity for an isotropic Gaussian initial law

Let X∼pdata∈𝒫+(E)X\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}\in\mathcal{P}_{+}(E) have finite second moment, and let Z∼pinit=𝒩(0,Id⁡)Z\sim p_{\mathrm{init}}=\mathcal N(0,\operatorname{Id}) be independent of XX. No Gaussian assumption is made on pdatap_{\mathrm{data}}. For Xt=(1−t)X+tZX_t=(1-t)X+tZ and 0<t<10<t<1, Gaussian convolution gives a smooth positive density. Differentiating its integral expression gives ∇pt(y)=−1t2∫E(y−(1−t)x)pdata(x)1tdpinit(y−(1−t)xt)dx,\nabla p_t(y) =-\frac1{t^2}\int_E\bigl(y-(1-t)x\bigr)p_{\mathrm{data}}(x) \frac1{t^d}p_{\mathrm{init}}\!(\frac{y-(1-t)x}{t})dx, and hence ∇log⁡pt(y)=−1t𝔼[Z∣Xt=y].\nabla\log p_t(y)=-\frac1t\mathbb E[Z\mid X_t=y]. This Gaussian score relation also appears in stochastic interpolants [3]. Combining it with Xt=(1−t)X+tZX_t=(1-t)X+tZ and vt(y)=𝔼[Z−X∣Xt=y]v_t(y)=\mathbb E[Z-X\mid X_t=y] yields vt(y)=−y+t∇log⁡pt(y)1−t=∇ϕt(y),ϕt(y)=−∥y∥22(1−t)−t1−tlog⁡pt(y).v_t(y)=-\frac{y+t\nabla\log p_t(y)}{1-t} =\nabla\phi_t(y),\qquad \phi_t(y)=-\frac{\|y\|^2}{2(1-t)} -\frac{t}{1-t}\log p_t(y). The field has finite L2(pt)L^2(p_t) norm under the second-moment assumption. It is therefore the gradient representative in the weighted Helmholtz decomposition and minimises the kinetic energy among fields representing ∂tpt\partial_t p_t [17]. This holds for every such pdatap_{\mathrm{data}}, including the Gaussian mixture used in our figures.

4.5 Geodesics in Wasserstein–Otto geometry

A constant-speed geodesic between pdatap_{\mathrm{data}} and pinitp_{\mathrm{init}} minimises the kinetic action. For endpoints with finite second moments, the Benamou–Brenier formula gives [5] W22(pdata,pinit)=inf∂tpt+div⁡(ptvt)=0p0=pdata,p1=pinit∫01∫E∥vt(y)∥2pt(y)dydt,\begin{equation} \label{eq:geodesic-minimum-action} W_2^2(p_{\mathrm{data}},p_{\mathrm{init}}) =\inf_{\substack{\partial_t p_t+\mathop{\mathrm{div}}(p_tv_t)=0\\ p_0=p_{\mathrm{data}},\ p_1=p_{\mathrm{init}}}} \int_0^1\int_E\|v_t(y)\|^2p_t(y)\,dy\,dt, \end{equation}(13) where the admissible pairs satisfy the continuity equation weakly and have finite action. If the geodesic density is smooth and positive and its minimum-energy velocity vtgeo=∇ϕtv_t^{\mathrm{geo}}=\nabla\phi_t is continuously differentiable in time and space, this velocity satisfies [17] ∂tvtgeo+(Dvtgeo)vtgeo=0.\begin{equation} \label{eq:geodesic-acceleration} \boxed{\, \partial_t v_t^{\mathrm{geo}} +(Dv_t^{\mathrm{geo}})v_t^{\mathrm{geo}}=0. \,} \end{equation}(14) Here DvtgeoDv_t^{\mathrm{geo}} is the spatial Jacobian matrix.

5 Discussion

5.1 Minimum-energy velocity and a nongeodesic path

For pinit=𝒩(0,Id⁡)p_{\mathrm{init}}=\mathcal N(0,\operatorname{Id}), the exact population Flow Matching field is a gradient and is therefore the minimum-energy representative of each density variation, as shown in Section 4.4. A learned network approximates this field. The density path can nevertheless have excess action. Take independent X,Z∼𝒩(0,Id⁡)X,Z\sim\mathcal N(0,\operatorname{Id}). Our interpolation formula gives pt=𝒩(0,((1−t)2+t2)Id⁡),vt(y)=2t−1(1−t)2+t2y.p_t=\mathcal N\!(0,\bigl((1-t)^2+t^2\bigr)\operatorname{Id}), \qquad v_t(y)=\frac{2t-1}{(1-t)^2+t^2}\,y. The Gaussian contracts up to t=1/2t=1/2 and then expands. Its WO energy is positive, whereas the geodesic between these identical endpoints is constant, with zero energy. 5.2 Straight trajectories and training towards optimal transport

For quadratic optimal transport in Euclidean space, particles along a Wasserstein geodesic follow affine trajectories Yt=(1−t)Y0+tY1Y_t=(1-t)Y_0+tY_1 [6]. For a smooth flow Ẏt=vθ(t,Yt)\dot Y_t=v_\theta(t,Y_t), this means Ÿt=[∂tvθ+(Dvθ)vθ](t,Yt)=0.\ddot Y_t= \bigl[\partial_t v_\theta+(Dv_\theta)v_\theta\bigr](t,Y_t)=0. A possible training regularisation is therefore ℒFM(θ)+λ∫01𝔼[∥∂tvθ(t,Yt)+(Dvθ(t,Yt))vθ(t,Yt)∥2]dt,λ>0,\mathcal L_{\mathrm{FM}}(\theta) +\lambda\int_0^1\mathbb E\![ \|\partial_t v_\theta(t,Y_t) +(Dv_\theta(t,Y_t))v_\theta(t,Y_t)\|^2]dt, \qquad \lambda>0, where the expectation is over trajectories of the learned flow. This encourages constant velocities along particles. In several dimensions, straightness alone does not select the quadratic optimal coupling. To target OT, endpoint matching and the gradient structure of the geodesic velocity must also be controlled [17].

The independent-pair loss prescribes a generally nongeodesic path, so the added constraint changes its population minimiser. Using an OT coupling for the training pairs makes the regression target compatible with displacement interpolation [22]. These choices matter both for transport cost and for numerical sampling: an exactly constant velocity along a trajectory allows a single explicit Euler step to reach its endpoint, which motivates learning straighter flows [7].
